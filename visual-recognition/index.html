For recognition,computer vision is ameasurement problem

Ziheng WuZhenghao Chen

Recognition begins in physical reality. Electronic acquisition turns that reality into a digital observation; computation turns its evidence into a report that others can interpret, test, and share.

A conventional recognizer often asks one parameterized map to do two jobs: make a non-ideal observation usable, then infer and report what it means. The coupling does not cause information loss. It hides whether the target was identifiable from that observation in the first place. A controlled measurement model makes this condition explicit.

Long-form research essay Approx. 24 min read 50-paper source base
The argument in one sentence

Visual recognition turns a digitally acquired observation of an existing physical state into a communicable linguistic or symbolic report that can participate in shared consensus; because acquisition determines which evidence exists, this process should be modeled as controlled measurement.

Scope

This essay develops the argument through recognition: estimating a query-defined property of the world and reporting it. The deeper boundary is reportability. Tracking and some low-level vision tasks fit when their target is an identity, trajectory, attribute, or structured estimate with a shared interpretation. Generation and reconstruction usually fall outside when their primary target is a new perceptual artifact rather than a reportable semantic object. They may still enter as tools that transform or propose evidence.

01

Start with a room

Imagine an observation room. The lighting is adequate. The background is clean. Resolution is sufficient. Nothing relevant is occluded. The observer can correct or calibrate the visual channel. For the moment, restrict the task to recognition.

The room is deliberately unlike the field. Recognition-oriented computer vision is an applied discipline of observing physical reality through electronic acquisition and computation. Uneven light, occlusion, finite resolution, motion, sensor noise, and limited access are not exceptions added after the clean problem. They are the native conditions that make the discipline necessary.

Place an object in the room and ask a question about it. What is the observer doing?

At the physical level, the observer receives a measurement of the world through a visual channel. At the task level, the observer estimates a property of that world: its category, identity, color, text, count, relation, or defect. If the result must travel to another person or system, the observer then expresses the estimate in a shared codebook.

This room is a thought experiment, not an engineering target. There is no single perfect photograph that every system must reconstruct. The room supplies a control condition: it removes nuisance factors long enough to expose a hidden assumption. A stable relation between physical reality and a communicable recognition result already depends on input conditions that preserve the evidence required by the question.

Those conditions are always relative to the question. A view may be sufficient to call an object a cup and still fail to reveal a hairline crack. A well-lit face may support identity recognition while remaining insufficient for a medical diagnosis. The target property decides which evidence matters.

Author's thought experiment

The "ideal observation" isolates a stability assumption. It is neither a canonical image nor a state that every input should reach.

02

What is recognition measuring?

Visual recognition turns a digitally acquired observation of an existing physical state into a report about a query-defined property. The report enters a shared linguistic or symbolic codebook, where another observer can interpret it, compare it with alternatives, and decide whether to agree. Consensus here means shared rules of interpretation, not identical wording or one universal token.

The digital observation need not be a two-dimensional photograph. It may be a 2-D raster, a video, a depth map, a multi-view record, a 3-D point set or volume, or another sampled visual structure. Each is a high-dimensional computational object produced by an acquisition channel. Vectorizing that object does not make its semantic meaning a Euclidean property of the raw coordinates.

The intuitive measurement is a distance between visual evidence and a report that people can share. In the era of large language models, language provides the broadest practical interface we have for open-ended recognition. It is tempting to say that a visual encoder maps an observation to the nearest representation in a common language space.

That intuition is useful, but it needs two corrections.

First, the target is rarely one token. "A red ceramic mug," "a red cup," and a structured category record may all be acceptable for the same query. Recognition should minimize a task-specific loss over an acceptable set of reports, not a Euclidean distance to one privileged string.

Semantic reporting loss
Lsem(ẑ, y; x) = infz ∈ Zy(x) dtask(ẑ, z)

The report is compared with an acceptable semantic class Zy(x), under a declared task loss.

Second, a language space says how an estimate can be expressed. It does not establish whether the current observation supports that estimate. Two different worlds can produce the same stored pixels. A language model may still prefer one answer because that answer is common in training data. The result can be correct, but it is a prior-driven guess rather than a recovery of missing evidence from this case.

This yields a composite process with four distinct operations:

1 Form An electronic channel samples a world state into a digital observation.
→
2 Acquire A policy changes which evidence is available.
→
3 Infer An encoder and decision rule estimate the target.
→
4 Report The estimate enters a shared codebook.

The definition and the modeling claim are different. Recognition is the physical-reality-to-report task. Controlled measurement is the proposed model for that task when observation formation and evidence sufficiency cannot be taken for granted.

Most recognition systems combine observation correction, inference, and reporting inside one function from a fixed digital record to an answer. That can be efficient when the record is adequate. It also hides which part failed when the answer is wrong.

A single map absorbs correction and meaning

A conventional recognition model often optimizes a single map, fθ(o, x) → z. It does not normally reconstruct an ideal image. Instead, the same encoder learns to tolerate or normalize changes in lighting, viewpoint, scale, blur, and occlusion while it also learns the semantic distinctions required by the label or report space. In effect, it makes the initial model-visible digital observation usable and decides what that observation means inside one objective.

Here a fixed or single observation means a fixed evidence source, not one forward pass. The model may reason repeatedly or compute many deterministic views of the same record. Unless it reveals a richer retained resource or measures the world again, every result remains a function of the original evidence.

This coupling is coherent when the observation retains enough information about the target. When different target states produce the same observation, no deterministic image-to-label map can recover both labels from that observation. A risk-minimizing predictor can still return a posterior, a common label, or a Bayes decision. It cannot turn that prediction into case-specific visual evidence.

Imprecise: coupling makes the problem unmeasurable
Precise: coupling hides whether the target is identifiable from the fixed observation, and whether another legal measurement could make it recoverable

The coupling did not create the information loss. It hid the condition under which recognition was identifiable. Missing evidence can then look like encoder error, acquisition failure can look like representation failure, and a prior-driven guess can look like a measured result.

03

A continuous history of recognition

This is a retrospective lens on recognition, not a history of computer vision as a whole. Earlier researchers did not use this vocabulary, and their work should not be rewritten as if they did. Within the narrower recognition lineage, a sustained concern has been how an available visual observation becomes a useful task representation.

As raster inputs grew in two spatial dimensions, CPU, memory, storage, and matching budgets remained modest. This made computation an explicit design pressure, though not the only motive. Handcrafted recognition pipelines made the representation choice visible. SIFT encoded selected image neighborhoods as invariant descriptors; HOG pooled local gradients over a dense grid. Both replaced direct pixel comparison with task-designed statistics, deciding which structure should survive into matching or a closed label space [4][24]. Calling this task-oriented compression is our retrospective systems reading, not the vocabulary of those papers.

CNNs learned more of that transformation. Local connectivity and shared weights were already central to early convolutional systems [3]. AlexNet showed what deep supervised recognition could do at ImageNet scale [5], while residual connections made much deeper encoders practical [6]. For each recognition case, the camera still supplied one fixed observation. The network learned how to compress it into a representation that served the declared labels.

Self-supervised learning and transformers broadened this representation. Contrastive pretraining reduced dependence on one class vocabulary [7]. Vision transformers changed the allocation of architectural bias and spatial interaction [8]. Neither development erased the boundary between evidence recorded by the sensor and regularity supplied by the training distribution.

Vision-language learning changed the report interface. CLIP connected images to a broad natural-language supervision space [9]. Visual instruction tuning connected image features to conversational language models [10]. These systems made far more recognition results expressible and transferable. They did not prove that language embeddings contain a universal semantic metric, nor that an answer with small embedding distance is supported by the pixels.

Across these changes, recognition systems have absorbed more of the route from a non-ideal digital observation to a report into one parameterized map. One might hope for a perfect parameter setting that covers every acquisition condition. The paper does not claim that such a map is impossible: when the fixed observation identifies the target, a sufficiently capable decoder may be enough. The boundary appears when target-distinct worlds produce the same record. No parameters can recover both case-specific answers from that record.

VLM agents add a second degree of freedom. A system can now choose a crop, reveal a high-resolution region, select a video frame, run an image operator, adjust a sensor, or move through the world. Recent systems support limited forms of model-directed visual planning in bounded environments: they select regions, construct visual workspaces, and append returned artifacts to a reasoning trace [25][26]. Other designs formalize crop selection or choices among acquire, answer, and abstain [17][18]. Most still operate on retained records or fixed tool libraries rather than remeasure the physical world, and a tool call alone does not establish evidence use or policy reliability. The important change is functional: recognition can optimize not only an encoder for one fixed observation, but also a closed-loop policy over which evidence to obtain before reporting.

Encoder paradigm
Given an observation, learn the best representation.
+
Measurement-policy paradigm
Given a question and a budget, learn which observation to obtain.
04

A fixed digital observation may not identify the target

Now remove the observation room. Increase exposure until details wash out. Reduce it until the image becomes nearly black. Blur the text. Hide the object behind an occluder. Compress a high-resolution record into a thumbnail.

At which point should a VLM still recognize the target?

There is no observer-independent answer. The threshold depends on the target, sensor, prior, loss, model family, available tools, and tolerated error. A category may remain identifiable after fine-grained text is gone. One encoder may recover weak contrast that another cannot. A retained RAW file may contain information absent from the rendered JPEG. A persistent scene may be measured again after the stored image has saturated.

Static robustness benchmarks remain useful. They measure the risk of a model under a declared corruption process and a fixed observation contract. The conceptual mistake is to treat every failure on the current model-visible digital observation as the same kind of failure.

Decoder failure

The evidence is present

A better encoder, representation, prompt, or derived view can make existing evidence accessible.

Policy failure

The evidence is reachable

A legal crop, retained frame, sensor action, or viewpoint could help, but the agent does not choose it.

Information failure

The evidence is unavailable

All legal traces leave target-distinct worlds indistinguishable. The correct response is abstention.

An end-to-end map does not cause these information limits. It mixes them into one training error. More parameters may reduce decoder error. More varied data may teach useful invariances. Neither operation can reconstruct case-specific information that the measurement channel destroyed. Once this distinction is explicit, "hard visual example" stops being one undifferentiated category.

05

The human clue is active sampling, not a perfect internal model

Humans do not learn every possible corrupted image before they can recognize an object. They also do not passively solve every view. They turn their eyes, move their head, change distance, seek light, refocus, rotate an object, or ask someone to reveal it. In harder observations, they replay a long video, jump between segments, compare views side by side, record intermediate findings, and return to unresolved details. From these operations they build higher-order strategies for search, comparison, memory, and verification. Many simple actions happen so quickly that the observation policy disappears from introspection.

Visual cognition supports this narrower claim. Yarbus's classic demonstrations showed that inspection patterns change with the task. Studies of natural behavior found that gaze is coordinated with ongoing action and often explained better by task demand than by a context-free salience map [11][12]. Bayesian models of visual search have treated fixation choice as information acquisition under a declared signal model and objective [13].

Neuroscience also argues against a false choice between invariant representation and active sensing. Population representations in the ventral pathway support tolerance to changes in position, scale, pose, illumination, and clutter [15]. Active sensing work emphasizes that movements of sensory organs structure the signal arriving at the brain [16]. Natural vision uses both mechanisms: extract stable information from the current view, then seek another view when the current one is insufficient.

This evidence does not prove that the brain implements the framework proposed here. It does not establish a unique ideal view or an optimal human scanpath. It supports the design question: why should a visual agent be forced to internalize every observation correction inside one static encoder when observation itself can be controlled? The question is now actionable because visual encoders and agents already have preliminary abilities to plan over crops, frames, and tools. Their next observation need not remain an external instruction.

Behavioral evidence

Gaze and inspection depend on the task.

Normative evidence

Performance bounds depend on the signal model, prior, loss, and target.

Neural evidence

Appearance tolerance and active sensing can coexist.

06

The central distinction: reporting distance and recovery cost

The original intuition speaks of distance to a language representation. That describes the final reporting problem. Non-ideal observation introduces a second quantity: the cost of reaching a trace that supports the target.

Output side

Semantic reporting loss

How close is the report to an acceptable expression of the target?

"Did the system say the right thing?"
≠
Observation side

Recovery cost

How much legal observation action is needed before the target is supported?

"Did the system obtain enough evidence?"

Let H denote the current observation trace. Define its best attainable conditional decision risk as ρ(H). A trace is sufficient for the query when that risk falls below a declared tolerance δ:

Gδ = { h : ρ(h) ≤ δ }

An observation policy does not need to recover one ideal image. It needs a low-cost path from the current trace into this sufficient set. Different paths may qualify. One may expose a retained high-resolution crop. Another may collect a new exposure. A third may move the object. The right path depends on the target and the authority granted to the agent.

This gives a risk-cost frontier. After a task declares constraints on its cost dimensions or an explicit scalarization, let B denote the resulting budget. For action set A, let r*A(B) be the lowest decision risk attainable by any valid policy and decoder. Under that convention, the minimum identification budget is the smallest B for which the risk crosses the task tolerance.

Information-limited frontier
C*A(δ) = inf { B ≥ 0 : r*A(B) ≤ δ }

This quantity is relative to the target, prior, loss, action set, cost convention, and budget. It is not an intrinsic score attached to an image.

The frontier also separates two engineering gaps. The model accessibility gap asks whether the decoder can use the evidence already acquired. The policy acquisition gap asks whether the agent obtained the best evidence available within budget. Encoder research mostly attacks the first. VLM-agent research must address both.

07

Model visual action by capability, provenance, and cost

A tool name is not an action model. The same command can do different work under different observation contracts: a crop may enlarge pixels already visible in a thumbnail, reveal the original high-resolution record, or steer a camera toward a new region. To decide whether an action is useful, a visual agent must represent what the action can do, where its evidence comes from, and what it costs now.

01 · Capability

What can it do?

Transform, access, sample, intervene, compare, aggregate, record, plan, or propose a hypothesis. Capabilities compose; they are not mutually exclusive tool classes.

02 · Provenance

Where did the evidence come from?

The current record, a richer retained resource, a new measurement, a measurement after intervention, or a prior-generated hypothesis.

03 · Cost

What must the observer spend?

Time, compute, memory, storage, bandwidth, energy, acquisition access, physical risk, or human effort.

Action signature
at = (operator, capabilities, evidence source, preconditions, expected gain, cost vector)

The history ht matters. Replaying one more frame may be cheap after a video is indexed and expensive when it must be fetched from a remote archive.

Capabilities compose into observation strategies

A difficult observation rarely reduces to one isolated operation. Reviewing a long video may require access to retained frames, temporal search, cross-segment comparison, note-taking, external memory, and a plan for which interval to inspect next. A physical inspection may combine viewpoint control, illumination, sensor focus, object manipulation, comparison with an earlier record, and a request for human assistance. The useful unit is the strategy over a trace, not the tool label.

transform access sample intervene compare aggregate record plan ask hypothesize

Provenance determines evidential status

A gamma correction can be extremely useful to a finite VLM because it may expose weak contrast in a form the model reads reliably. Yet if it is computed only from the original pixels, it cannot distinguish two worlds that already produced exactly the same record. It is post-processing of the parent observation [2]. A crop that queries an original-resolution file is different: it can reveal case information hidden from the initial thumbnail. Remeasurement goes further by interrogating the persistent world again. A generative completion has a different status again: it is a hypothesis from learned priors until a case-linked measurement confirms it.

These are provenance labels, not a complete taxonomy of visual behavior. They prevent transformed or generated detail from being silently counted as newly measured evidence while allowing either operation to improve model access or guide the next measurement.

A benchmark may constrain some dimensions and optimize others, or compare policies on a Pareto frontier. It should not assume that one crop, one camera movement, and one minute of human inspection all have the same unit cost. Any scalar reward must state whose costs it represents and how the dimensions were weighted.

08

The hard boundary is indistinguishability under allowed action

Consider two physical states with different target values. Suppose every legal policy, under the available budget, produces the same distribution of visible traces in both states. No decision rule can reliably distinguish them from those traces.

This is the irrecoverable boundary. It is not a brightness threshold, an embedding radius, or a surface in pixel space. It is an equivalence class defined by the target, measurement operators, action set, and budget. Change the agent's authority and the boundary can move.

The information limit

With two equally likely target states and binary loss, let ΔB be the greatest total-variation separation that any legal policy can produce between their trace distributions. The best possible error is

error* = (1 − ΔB) / 2.

If ΔB = 0, even an unrestricted decoder has error 1/2. If one measurement makes the distributions differ, the optimum can improve.

A white image therefore has no intrinsic status. If saturation collapsed several targets to the same stored record and the agent can only process those pixels, the targets are irrecoverable. If the scene persists and the agent can lower the camera exposure, the same initial view may be recoverable. The visual input did not change its pixels; the observation contract changed its available experiments.

This boundary also explains why scaling alone is insufficient. A larger model can close an accessibility gap. It cannot split worlds that remain observationally equivalent under every legal trace. Fluency after information loss is still fluency.

09

The post-training unit should be a measurement episode

An observation-answer pair can verify whether a final string matches a label. It cannot tell us whether the available evidence supported the answer, whether another measurement was needed, whether a tool result mattered, or whether the agent spent its budget sensibly.

This is already visible in recent VLM-agent work. LFPC reports that models can form an answer before cropping and use the crop only formally; its training setup creates an information gap so the later crop must carry useful evidence [17]. Perception-R1 separates the correctness of visual statements in a reasoning trace from final-answer reward [19]. BCEA adds an acquire option to answer and abstain under a budgeted reliability contract [18]. These systems solve different parts of the same problem. None, by itself, supplies a general measurement contract.

The proposed training record is a measurement episode. It contains the initial world or retained resource, the question, target property, legal action set, budget, observation trace, provenance graph, stopping decision, report, and verifier information.

world state query + target initial observation actions + budget trace + provenance stop / answer / abstain privileged verifier

The evaluator then keeps three questions separate:

  1. Truth

    Does the report agree with the target property?

  2. Support

    Does the trace contain case-linked evidence for that report?

  3. Policy

    Were acquisition, stopping, and abstention appropriate under the budget?

A lucky answer can pass the first test and fail the second. A correct, supported answer can still waste ten unnecessary tool calls and fail the third. If reward compresses all three into one scalar too early, the model can exploit whichever signal is easiest. Benchmarks should report the components separately even when training combines them.

Counterfactual checks make support more concrete. Remove the decisive evidence: the agent should seek another measurement, lower confidence, or abstain unless redundant evidence remains. Swap the target evidence while preserving nuisance context: the answer should change. Replace a measured crop with an irrelevant one: a tool-dependent answer should react. Provenance says where an artifact came from; these interventions test whether the decision relied on it.

10

A controlled-measurement model for recognition

The controlled measurement model

A recognition system receives a query about a target property of a world state. Under an explicit set of measurement operators, action capabilities, provenance rules, multidimensional costs, and a finite budget, it builds a provenance-bearing observation trace. It reports through a shared codebook when that trace is sufficient, continues observing when a legal action is expected to improve the decision enough to justify its cost, and abstains when the target remains unsupported or action-relative recovery is impossible.

This is a modeling extension for non-ideal recognition, not a replacement definition for computer vision. It retains the encoder. Representation learning remains necessary because evidence must still be compressed, compared, and expressed efficiently. The change is architectural and evaluative: the encoder no longer bears sole responsibility for every observation condition. It becomes one part of a system that can also control observation.

Recognition is the cleanest case, not the only possible application. A tracker can report identity and trajectory; a low-level system can report an image property or a structured estimate when the contract fixes how that output is interpreted. A generator whose primary target is a new image poses a different problem. The relevant boundary is whether the target has a shared semantic reporting contract, not whether an output can merely be serialized.

The definition also clarifies what is not being claimed:

  • This essay develops the theory through recognition. It extends to tracking and low-level targets only when the output has a shared reporting contract.
  • There is no universal ideal image.
  • Language is a broad reporting interface, not a source of visual identifiability.
  • Active perception, controlled sensing, and POMDPs already contain much of the mathematical structure [1][21][23].
  • A visual tool is not automatically informative, and more tools do not guarantee a better learned policy.
  • Robotics is one route to new measurements, not the only one. Retained high-resolution resources, video, simulators, controllable sensors, and multi-view data also expand observation authority.

What would make this a research program?

A paradigm needs claims that can fail. The following hypotheses turn the essay into an empirical agenda.

H1

At matched data and compute, explicit measurement episodes will improve calibrated evidence acquisition over answer-only post-training.

H2

Separating model accessibility from policy acquisition will predict failures that aggregate accuracy and corruption severity hide.

H3

Provenance-aware counterfactual evaluation will expose correct but unsupported visual answers that final-answer reward accepts.

H4

Agents trained with answer, continue, stop, and abstain as distinct actions will learn better risk-cost frontiers than agents rewarded for tool use alone.

These are not theorems. Explicit modularity may fail to beat a well-designed end-to-end system. Provenance may be costly. Verifying evidential support may require privileged simulator state that real data does not provide. Some tasks may be served perfectly well by a static encoder. The framework earns its place only if it yields cleaner diagnoses, better calibrated policies, or more efficient learning under controlled comparison.

11

The question changes when the observer can act

Conventional recognition asks: given this fixed observation, what is it?

A visual measurement system asks a larger question: given this target, this evidence, this authority, and this budget, what should I observe next, and when do I know enough to report?

The difference matters whenever the current observation is not stable or sufficient. The system may need to re-express recorded evidence, reveal a richer retained resource, compare observations, externalize intermediate findings, measure the world again, intervene on the scene, or admit that the boundary cannot be crossed. These operations combine different capabilities, evidence sources, and costs.

Recognition research has spent decades learning better maps from a given observation to a task representation. VLM agents make it possible to learn a second map: from the current trace and query to the next observation action. The two maps may share parameters and still be trained end to end. Their conceptual separation matters because only the second can change the observation contract. Together they give us a cleaner way to define visual failure, a stricter way to verify visual reasoning, and a concrete post-training target for systems that can choose how they look.
